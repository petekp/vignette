# Composing a stitch a model can read (2026-09-17)

Pete: "Look into the most legible way to compose a stitched image for an LLM (vertical or grid
layout, gap and padding, badge size and placement, separators, downscaling limits, whether a label
per piece helps) and let that inform how `Stitch.swift` composes the output."

The finding that matters most is not a layout: **a stitch of three 1760 x 1080 screenshots reaches
a standard-tier reader at 45% of its size, so 12 px UI text arrives 5.4 px tall.** Six pieces make
it 3.8 px. Nothing about gap, badge, or grid rescues that. When the screenshots carry text the
model has to read, the pieces should go as separate images — Cmd+C on the selection already copies
all of them as files, and chat apps attach them one by one. The stitch is for when one image is
what is wanted: a sequence to show at a glance, a before and after, a drop onto something that
takes a single file.

Everything below is what the composition does with the budget it has.

## What a reader does to an image

Retrieved 2026-09-17.

**Anthropic** ([vision](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/vision)): "Claude
views images in patches instead of pixels. Each patch is a 28×28-pixel block of the image, referred
to as a visual token. An image, therefore, costs `⌈width / 28⌉ × ⌈height / 28⌉` visual tokens."
Each tier has two limits: the standard tier (every model before
Claude 4.7) allows a **long edge of 1568 px and 1568 visual tokens**, the high-resolution tier
2576 px and 4784. "When an image is downsized, Claude scales it to the largest size that
fits the tier's limits while preserving its aspect ratio." Maximum dimensions are 8000 x 8000 px,
and a request with more than 20 images drops every image to 2000 px. The quality guidance is
explicit about this use: "If the image contains important text, make sure it's legible and not too
small", and "Take into account that your image might be resized if it is too large; this might, for
example, make text less legible. Consider pre-resizing your images, cropping them, or both."

**OpenAI**
([images and vision](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/images-vision)): the tile scheme
that still applies to the gpt-4o/4.1/5.x family scales an image "to fit in a 2048px x 2048px
square", then scales the shortest side to 768 px, then counts 512 px tiles. The newer models count
32 x 32 patches instead, up to 2500 of them at `detail: high`. Its limitations section says "Small
text: Enlarge text within the image to improve readability."

**Google** ([image
understanding](https://ai.google.dev/gemini-api/docs/image-understanding)): "Larger images are tiled
into 768x768 pixel tiles, each costing 258 tokens", with a crop unit of roughly
`floor(min(width, height) / 1.5)`. Firebase's [input file
requirements](https://firebase.google.com/docs/ai-logic/input-file-requirements) add that larger
images "are scaled down and padded to fit a maximum resolution of 3072 x 3072".

Anthropic's standard tier is the smallest of the three, so a composition that reads well there
reads well for the others, and it is what `Stitch.readerScale` models. It reproduces the sizes the
vision docs tabulate: 1920 x 1080 comes out 1456 x 819 at 1560 tokens, 3840 x 2160 the same, and
2000 x 1500 comes out 1267 x 951 against the doc's 1269 x 952 — two pixels, same token count.

On separate images versus one composite there is one piece of research worth citing and it points
the other way from stitching: MIRB ([arXiv
2406.12742](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.12742), section 5.2) tested exactly this and found that
"most models are worse with this encoding of concatenating images", the exception being models
trained on single images only. It is from June 2024 and its tasks are not screenshot reading, so it
is a reason to prefer separate images, not proof.

## What the budget does to a stitch

Two limits at once — a long edge of 1568 px and 1.23 megapixels of patches — mean a composition can
only use the whole pixel budget while its aspect stays inside about 2:1. Past that the long edge is
capped first and the rest of the budget is wasted. That is the whole argument for a grid.

Pieces of 1760 x 1080, the usual browser window on a Retina display:

| pieces | layout | composed | reader scale | 12 px text becomes | a vertical stack would be |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 (not stitched) | — | 1760x1080 | 0.79 | 9.5 px | — |
| 2 | 1 column | 1804x2226 | 0.54 | 6.5 px | 0.54 |
| 3 | 1 column | 1804x3328 | 0.45 | 5.4 px | 0.45 |
| 4 | 2 columns | 3586x2226 | 0.39 | 4.7 px | 0.35 |
| 5 | 2 columns | 3586x3328 | 0.32 | 3.8 px | 0.28 |
| 6 | 2 columns | 3586x3328 | 0.32 | 3.8 px | 0.24 |

So the grid is worth about a tenth more resolution at four pieces and about a third more at six,
and nothing at two or three, where the stack is already inside the budget's shape. The same column counts win on
Anthropic's high-resolution tier, where the numbers are much kinder (three pieces at 0.77, six at
0.56), so choosing the layout against the standard tier costs nothing and is safe for every reader.

## The rules

`Stitch.layout` tries every column count and keeps the one whose composition survives the reader's
resize best, fewest columns first. Pieces run in reading order, left to right and top to bottom, so
badge 1 is top left. A column is as wide as its widest piece, a row as tall as its tallest, and a
piece sits in the middle of its cell.

Gap and padding are one number: **2% of the smallest piece's short side, bounded to 12–48 px**
(22 px for a 1080 px piece, near the 24 px that was there before). A fixed gap is a hairline on a
5K stitch and a margin on a small crop, and after the reader's resize a 24 px gap on a six-piece
stitch is 7 px — a line, not a separation. The outer padding is the same number, so the rhythm is
uniform. No drawn separators: the near-black ground between pieces is the separator, and it is the
one thing in the picture that is never part of a screenshot.

A badge is **6% of its own piece's short side, bounded to 32–128 px** (65 px on a 1080 px piece,
against the fixed 44 px before), the number drawn at 60% of that, inset from the piece's top-left
corner by a quarter of the badge, and never wider than 80% of that short side — a badge needs 1.25
times its diameter to sit in, so the 32 px floor alone would push it off a piece under 40 px. The
point of the fraction is that the badge keeps its size relative to the UI text beside it whatever
the reader does: about five times the height of 12 px text, at any scale. In absolute terms the badge on a six-piece stitch arrives 21 px tall where the
old fixed one arrived 14 px.

The badge stays inside its own piece rather than hanging into the gap beside it: in a grid the gap
is narrower than a badge, so a badge that hung into it would land on the neighbouring screenshot.
The cost is that it covers the top-left corner of its own piece, as it did before.

**No text label per piece.** Anthropic's own advice for several images is to "introduce each one
with a short text label (`Image 1:`, `Image 2:`, and so on) so you can refer to them by name" — in
a composite the badge is that label, and it is already the number the card's selection circle
showed, so the person and the model are counting the same way. A file name under each piece would
cost a row of pixels to say `Screenshot 2026-09-17 at 8.45.51 AM.png`, which names nothing the
prompt does not.

**No downscaling below the composition's own size**, except one limit: `ui.stitchLongSide`, 4096 px
by default, is where the composition stops growing. The stitched PNG is a file the person keeps as
well as pastes, so throwing away resolution they did not ask to lose is not the app's business; but
above 4096 px nothing is gained, because every reader in the list resizes below that anyway, while
the file gets slow to paste and can cross the 10 MB upload limit. The composition is drawn straight
at the output size rather than drawn large and resampled: a six-piece 5K stitch would otherwise
need a buffer of several hundred megabytes.

The `[stitch] ok` line reports what the composition costs, so a script or a person can see it
without measuring. `readerScale` is end to end, `ui.stitchLongSide` included: the cap and the
reader's own resize both count, because both of them are between the screenshot and the model.

```
[stitch] ok /…/Stitch 2026-09-17 at 7.58.02 PM.png from 3 images, 1804x3328 columns=1 readerScale=0.45 1512345 bytes, copied
```

## Verified

Six fixtures in the scratch folder (1760x1080, 1200x800, 900x1400, 1760x1080, 600x400, 2400x1000),
stitched through `vignette://stitch`:

```
[stitch] ok …/Stitch 2026-09-17 at 9.58.15 PM.png from 2 images, 1792x1928 columns=1 readerScale=0.59 72150 bytes, copied
[stitch] ok …/Stitch 2026-09-17 at 9.58.21 PM.png from 3 images, 1792x3344 columns=1 readerScale=0.45 123754 bytes, copied
[stitch] ok …/Stitch 2026-09-17 at 9.58.27 PM.png from 6 images, 4096x1906 columns=3 readerScale=0.29 157638 bytes, copied
```

The six-piece composition would have been 5408 x 2516 in three columns; `ui.stitchLongSide` brought
it to 4096 x 1906, which is the width the file has, and that cap is the difference between the
0.38 a reader's resize leaves of the capped picture and the 0.29 the line reports, which is what a
reader leaves of the screenshots. Read back at three times its size, badge 1 on the first piece is
a 65 px disc with a white number, inset 16 px from the piece's corner.

The same three runs with a file that is not an image among them report `from 2 images`: a piece
that will not decode is dropped, and the line counts what is in the picture.

## Where the numbers live

`Stitch.swift`: `readerLongSide`, `readerTokens`, and `readerPatch` are the reader's published
limits, and `gapFraction`, `gapRange`, `badgeFraction`, and `badgeRange` follow from the arithmetic
above. None of them is a preference, so none is a setting. `ui.stitchLongSide` is the one a person
would move — someone who pastes into a reader with a bigger budget, or who wants a smaller file —
so it is a tweak with a slider, bounded 512–8192 in the panel and refused below 512 in the file:
under that a stitch is not a smaller picture but a useless one, four wide captures at 64 coming out
a 64 x 1 PNG the app still calls ok.
